Granada:
Andalusia's Moorish crown

9 min read  ·  Long-weekend itinerary

Perched at the foot of the Sierra Nevada, Granada is one of Europe's most layered cities — a place where the last great Moorish palace looks down over a tapas bar where the wine is still free, and where flamenco echoes through cave dwellings carved into the hillside before dawn.

Why Granada?

Few cities in Europe carry history as visibly as Granada. The Alhambra — that vast, honeyed-red palace-fortress commanding the ridge above the city — is one of the supreme achievements of Islamic architecture anywhere in the world. But the Alhambra is only the beginning. Below it, the whitewashed lanes of the Albaicín quarter tumble down toward a cathedral built on the foundations of a mosque, and the whole city still lives in the creative collision of three cultures: Moorish, Christian, and Jewish.

Granada also does something no other major Spanish city does quite so reliably: it gives you free tapas with every drink. Order a glass of local Alhambra beer or a glass of tinto de verano and the bar will send out a small plate — perhaps a wedge of tortilla, a skewer of serrano-wrapped melon, or a mound of patatas bravas. Eat and drink your way through an evening in the Realejo and the bill will be startlingly modest.

Essential tip

Alhambra tickets sell out weeks — sometimes months — in advance. Book online at alhambra-patronato.es as soon as your dates are confirmed. The Nasrid Palaces section has a fixed entry time slot; missing it means missing the heart of the complex. Early morning slots (first entry at 8:30 am) offer the best light and thinner crowds.

The neighbourhoods

Granada's character shifts block by block. Each barrio has its own mood and its own reason to linger — from hilltop cave bars to shaded Renaissance courtyards — and the city is compact enough to walk between all of them in a single day.

Albaicín

The ancient Moorish quarter climbs the hill opposite the Alhambra in a maze of steep cobbled lanes, whitewashed carmenes (walled garden-houses), and hidden miradors. The Mirador de San Nicolás offers the city's most celebrated view — the Alhambra against the snow-capped Sierra Nevada.

Half-day minimum

Sacromonte

Carved into the tufa hillside east of the Albaicín, Sacromonte's cave dwellings have been home to the city's Romani community for centuries. This is Granada's flamenco heartland — zambra performances in the caves here are among the most authentic in Andalusia.

Evening & night

Realejo

The former Jewish quarter spreads south of the cathedral in a grid of quieter streets lined with tapas bars, independent shops, and the occasional crumbling palace. The Campo del Príncipe square is its social heart — perfect for an afternoon beer and free tapa in the shade.

Tapas crawl territory

Centro

The bustling lower city clusters around the Cathedral and the Alcaicería — the old Moorish silk market, now rebuilt and filled with craft stalls. The nearby Bib-Rambla square is Granada's main gathering place: café tables spill onto the plaza in the shadow of the cathedral's looming façade.

Cathedral & shopping

When to go

March, April, and October are Granada at its finest. Spring brings almond blossom in the foothills and mild temperatures ideal for walking the Alhambra gardens and the Albaicín lanes. October offers warm afternoons, clear skies, and the first snow glinting on the Sierra Nevada peaks above the city — one of the most unlikely and beautiful contrasts in European travel.

Summer (July and August) is extremely hot — daytime temperatures regularly exceed 38°C — but the evenings come alive, the terraces stay open until 3am, and the city's Festival Internacional de Música y Danza fills the Alhambra with concerts under the stars. Winter is cold and sometimes wet, but the ski resort at Sierra Nevada is only 45 minutes away, and the city is utterly your own.

Food & drink

Granada's food culture is built around the tapa — more generous and more serious here than almost anywhere else in Spain. The tradition of the free tapa lives on in the older bars around the cathedral and in the Realejo: at Bar Los Diamantes, a paper cone of fresh-fried prawns arrives with your first beer; at Bodegas Castañeda, a barrel-aged Rioja comes with a board of ibérico and manchego. The key is to keep moving: two drinks and two tapas per bar, then find the next place.

Beyond tapas, Granada's signature dish is plato alpujarreño — a hearty plate of fried eggs, black pudding, chorizo, ham, and potatoes that sustains hikers descending from the mountains. For something lighter, try the city's take on the Arab-influenced pastries still sold in tea houses around the Albaicín: honey-soaked pasteles de Gloria and crunchy almendrados made with Granada's own almonds.

Budget guide

Granada is one of Spain's most affordable cities. With the free tapa tradition, food costs are significantly lower than Madrid or Barcelona. Budget travellers can eat extremely well on €30–50 per day. Mid-range visitors staying in boutique hotels in the Albaicín should budget €100–160 per day all-in. The Alhambra ticket (around €19) is the main single cost — budget for it separately and book early.
