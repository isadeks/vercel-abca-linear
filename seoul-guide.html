Seoul:
South Korea's electric capital

10 min read  ·  Four-day itinerary

Seoul is a city of striking contradictions — ancient palaces shadowed by glass towers, Buddhist temples tucked behind neon-lit alleys, and a street food culture so vibrant it spills out of every covered market and lane. Nowhere else in Asia compresses so many centuries into a single, utterly contemporary metropolis.

Why Seoul?

Somewhere between Tokyo's precision and Hong Kong's energy, Seoul occupies a category entirely its own. The city was largely rebuilt after the Korean War and has transformed, in fewer than seventy years, into one of the world's most dynamic capitals — a centre of tech, design, film, music, and food that draws visitors from every continent.

What surprises most first-time visitors is how liveable it feels. The public transport is among the finest anywhere, the streets are safe and walkable, and English signage is widespread enough that navigation rarely becomes a barrier. Come in spring for the cherry blossoms, autumn for the foliage across the palace gardens, or winter for the stark beauty of snow-capped Bukhansan — the granite mountain that rises directly from the city's northern edge.

Practical tip

The T-money card is essential — a reloadable transit card that works on the subway, buses, and even taxis across Seoul and most of South Korea. Pick one up at any convenience store (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven) and top it up as needed. The subway system runs until around 1 am, is colour-coded and fully bilingual, and will take you within comfortable walking distance of almost every major sight.

The neighbourhoods

Seoul rewards a district-by-district approach. Each neighbourhood has a distinct personality shaped by its history — from the royal grandeur of Jongno to the indie creativity of Hongdae and the quiet elegance of Bukchon's preserved hanok (traditional courtyard house) streets. The Han River divides the city into two halves; Gangnam to the south is a world of high-rise luxury and global brands, while the northern districts hold most of the historical sites and the most atmospheric old-city texture.

Bukchon Hanok Village

A preserved neighbourhood of over 900 traditional hanok houses on the hillside between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung palaces. Early morning, before tour groups arrive, the stone-paved lanes are nearly empty and the city below seems to disappear.

Morning essential

Insadong

Seoul's traditional arts quarter — a winding street lined with craft galleries, teahouses, and antique dealers, branching into covered alleys full of ceramics, calligraphy, and handmade paper goods. The Sunday pedestrian market is particularly lively.

Afternoon browse

Hongdae

The creative district around Hongik University — indie cafés, vintage clothing stores, street murals, and a live music scene that runs from afternoon until well past midnight. Seoul's most youthful and spontaneous neighbourhood.

Evening district

Gyeongbokgung

The "Palace Greatly Blessed by Heaven" — the largest of Seoul's five grand palaces, restored to a fraction of its original 500 buildings. The changing of the royal guard ceremony runs twice daily at the main gate and is one of the city's most photographed spectacles.

Morning visit recommended

When to go

Spring (late March to early May) brings Seoul's famous cherry blossoms — Yeouido's riverside parks, Namsan, and the university campuses transform with a brief, luminous bloom that the entire city celebrates. Autumn (October to early November) offers crisp days and vivid foliage, particularly striking in the palace gardens and Bukhansan National Park.

Summer (June to August) is hot, humid, and occasionally heavy with monsoon rain — but the city barely slows down, and the rooftop bars and Han River parks are at their most animated. Winter is cold and dry, with occasional snow; the city's Christmas-to-New-Year atmosphere is warm and festive, and crowds at the major sights thin considerably.

Where to stay

The Jongno and Insadong districts place you within walking distance of the old city's major palaces and markets — ideal for first-time visitors who want to experience the historic heart of Seoul. Myeongdong, directly south of Namsan, is the most central and commercially active district, with hotels at every price point and unbeatable access to shopping, street food, and transport. For a more local experience, several beautifully restored hanok guesthouses operate in Bukchon, offering traditional floor sleeping, shared courtyard gardens, and a quiet that feels genuinely removed from the city below.

Budget guide

Budget travellers in a hostel or guesthouse can get by on ₩50,000–80,000 (€35–55) per day. A comfortable mid-range experience — business hotel, restaurant meals, museum entries — runs ₩150,000–250,000 (€105–175) per day. High-end hotels in Gangnam or the city centre typically start at ₩300,000–600,000 (€210–420) per night. Palace entry is ₩3,000 (€2) each; the integrated palace ticket (₩10,000) covers Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Deoksugung, and Changgyeonggung.

Food & drink

Seoul's food culture is one of the world's great culinary pleasures — from late-night pojangmacha (street food tent stalls) serving tteokbokki and hotteok to multi-storey restaurants specialising in a single dish refined over generations. Korean barbecue is the obvious starting point, but the city's range extends far beyond: banchan-laden home cooking in market neighbourhoods, cold buckwheat naengmyeon noodles on sweltering summer days, and a contemporary fine-dining scene that has earned global recognition.

Gwangjang Market, open since 1905, is Seoul's most atmospheric food destination — a covered hall where stalls sell bindaetteok (mung bean pancakes), mayak gimbap (sesame-rolled rice rolls), and raw beef yukhoe to a standing crowd that doesn't thin from morning to midnight. For coffee, Seoul's café culture rivals Tokyo's — the Ikseon-dong and Seongsu neighbourhoods in particular are home to some of the most considered and design-forward cafés in Asia.
