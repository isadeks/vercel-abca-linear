Marrakesh:
the red city

8 min read  ·  Long-weekend itinerary

Marrakesh assaults the senses from the moment you step through the medina gate — a labyrinth of souks, riads, and centuries-old mosques wrapped in the warm ochre glow of its famous rose-red walls.

Why Marrakesh?

Few cities in the world deliver such an immediate and overwhelming sense of place. Marrakesh — founded in 1062 by the Almoravid dynasty — has been a crossroads of Saharan trade routes, Berber culture, and Arab scholarship for nearly a thousand years. The old medina, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, preserves a medieval street plan that remains largely unchanged: narrow derbs (alleyways) open without warning onto grand squares, hidden gardens, and artisan quarters where craftspeople work as their great-grandparents did.

The city rewards slow exploration. A long weekend is enough to scratch the surface; a week begins to reveal its depths.

Practical tip

Stay inside or immediately adjacent to the medina. The atmosphere after the tour groups leave in the evening — lanterns lit, the call to prayer echoing across rooftops — is the real Marrakesh. Riad guesthouses are typically better value and more atmospheric than chain hotels in the Nouvelle Ville.

The neighbourhoods

The medina divides naturally into a few distinct zones, each with its own character.

Jemaa el-Fna

The great square at the medina's heart transforms through the day — orange-juice sellers and snake charmers by morning, a vast open-air food theatre by night. It is the city's living room.

Start here

The Souks

The network of covered markets north of the square is organised by trade: spice merchants, leather tanners, carpet weavers, metalworkers. Allow at least a half-day to get productively lost.

Half-day minimum

Mouassine

The most elegant quarter of the medina, with restored riads, boutique concept stores, and the beautiful Mouassine fountain and mosque. Quieter and more navigable than the central souks.

Afternoon stroll

Mellah

The historic Jewish quarter, now predominantly Muslim, clusters around the Rahba Kedima spice square. The covered Mellah market sells olives, preserved lemons, and dried fruits in extraordinary variety.

Morning market

What to see

The Bahia Palace is the city's most accessible great building — its painted cedar ceilings, zellij tilework, and shaded orange-tree courtyards take a full morning to appreciate. The Saadian Tombs nearby are smaller but astonishing: a mausoleum hidden for centuries and rediscovered in 1917, its chambers decorated with some of the finest stucco work in North Africa.

The Jardin Majorelle — created by French painter Jacques Majorelle and later saved by Yves Saint Laurent — offers a respite from the medina's intensity: cobalt-blue buildings, towering bamboo groves, and a excellent Berber museum. Arrive at opening time to beat the crowds.

When to go

October to April is ideal. Spring (March–May) brings warm days and occasional blossom in the palace gardens; autumn (September–November) is reliably sunny with manageable crowds. Avoid July and August — daytime temperatures frequently exceed 40 °C and the medina loses much of its local life to summer migration.

Ramadan is a fascinating time to visit if you're prepared to be flexible: many restaurants operate reduced daytime hours, but the nights come alive with family gatherings, lantern-lit street food, and an atmosphere unlike any other time of year.

Where to stay

A riad — a traditional courtyard house — is the definitive Marrakesh accommodation. The best are clustered in the Mouassine and Bab Doukkala neighbourhoods of the medina. Prices range from modest (€50–80 per night for a simple room around a tiled courtyard) to extravagant (€500+ for a fully restored palace with a plunge pool). Book well in advance for peak season; many riads have only four to six rooms.

Budget guide

Budget travellers can manage on €50–70 per day covering a simple riad, street food from Jemaa el-Fna, and entrance fees. Mid-range visitors spending €120–200 per day unlock proper riad accommodation and sit-down restaurant dinners. Luxury riads and fine dining push the daily cost to €300–500+.

Food & drink

Moroccan cuisine is among the most complex in the world — slow-cooked tagines of lamb and preserved lemon, bastilla (pigeon or chicken pastilla dusted with icing sugar), harira soup, and msemen flatbreads from street carts. The food stalls of Jemaa el-Fna are touristy but fun; for a more considered meal, the riad restaurants of Mouassine offer inventive modern Moroccan cooking in beautiful settings.

Morocco is a Muslim-majority country — alcohol is available in licensed restaurants and upmarket riads, but not universally. Fresh-squeezed orange juice (the Jemaa el-Fna vendors are legendary), mint tea served theatrically from a great height, and avocado smoothies are the drinks of the medina.
