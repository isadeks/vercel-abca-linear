Marrakesh:
city of colour and craft

8 min read  ·  One-week itinerary

Marrakesh is a city that overwhelms the senses in the best possible way — the din of the souks, the scent of rose water and cumin, riads hidden behind unmarked doorways, and a central square that reinvents itself every evening as one of the world's great open-air spectacles.

Why Marrakesh?

Few cities in the world pack so much atmosphere into such a compact space. The medina — a UNESCO World Heritage Site — is a labyrinth of narrow lanes organised by craft: the dyers' souk, the leather tanneries, the carpet merchants and spice sellers. Getting lost here is half the point. Around every corner is a tiled fountain, a crumbling riad doorway, or a craftsman at work.

Marrakesh is also a natural gateway to the wider country: the Atlas Mountains begin just an hour's drive away, and with more time you can push south towards the Sahara dunes at Merzouga. But the city itself rewards slow exploration — one week is enough to scratch the surface, two weeks to start feeling at home.

Practical tip

Download an offline map before you enter the medina — mobile signal can be patchy in the tightest alleys, and addresses are often approximate. The main souks are broadly safe in the day; stick to well-lit streets after dark. Bargaining is expected in the souks: start at around 30–40% of the asking price and work up slowly.

Highlights of the medina

A week in Marrakesh is best spent dipping in and out of the medina at different times of day. The Djemaa el-Fna square is electric at dusk — give yourself at least one evening lingering over the food stalls, trying harira soup, lamb skewers, and freshly squeezed orange juice. The Saadian Tombs and the Bahia Palace are both worth a morning each. The Ben Youssef Madrasa, a 14th-century Islamic college dripping in carved stucco and cedar, is among the finest examples of Moorish architecture in North Africa.

Djemaa el-Fna

The beating heart of Marrakesh transforms from a fruit-juice market at noon into a vast open-air theatre at dusk — storytellers, musicians, snake charmers, and dozens of food stalls.

Visit at dusk, then stay for dinner

The Souks

A network of covered lanes behind the square, organized by trade. Leather bags in one quarter, lanterns in the next, then spices, textiles, and handmade ceramics. Go with time and no agenda.

Allow a half-day minimum

Majorelle Garden

A vivid cobalt-and-yellow botanical garden created by French painter Jacques Majorelle, later restored by Yves Saint Laurent. A cool, serene counterpoint to the medina's chaos.

Book tickets in advance

Atlas Day Trips

The High Atlas rises just an hour from the city. Visit the Ourika Valley for waterfalls and Berber villages, or hike to the summit of Toubkal — North Africa's highest peak.

Best April–October

When to go

March to May and September to November offer the most pleasant conditions — warm days, cool evenings, and manageable crowds. Summers (June–August) are intensely hot, with midday temperatures regularly exceeding 38 °C; the medina traps heat and is exhausting to explore in the afternoon. Winters are mild and largely crowd-free, though evenings can be surprisingly cold in the mountains.

Ramadan brings a different rhythm to the city — many restaurants close during daylight hours, but the evenings come alive with iftar celebrations and a festive atmosphere that is genuinely special to witness.

Where to stay

Staying in a riad — a traditional townhouse built around a central courtyard — is the quintessential Marrakesh experience. Many have been beautifully restored, with plunge pools, rooftop terraces, and just four to ten rooms. The best riads book out months ahead in high season. The medina is the most atmospheric base; the Guéliz neighbourhood (the French-built new town) is quieter and more modern if you prefer wider streets and easier navigation.

Budget travellers will find good hostels clustered near Djemaa el-Fna. Mid-range riads in the northern medina offer excellent value without the premium price tag of the most famous addresses near the square.

Budget guide

Marrakesh is one of the most affordable city breaks in the Mediterranean region. Budget travellers can eat and sleep well for MAD 400–600 (£30–45) per day. A decent riad room starts at around MAD 600–900 (£45–70) per night. Mid-range visitors spending MAD 1,200–2,000 (£90–150) per day can stay in characterful boutique riads, take guided tours, and eat at Marrakesh's excellent French-Moroccan fusion restaurants.

Food & drink

Moroccan food is among the most distinctive in the world — slow-cooked tagines of lamb and preserved lemon, fluffy couscous piled with seven vegetables, bastilla (a sweet-savoury pigeon or chicken pie dusted with icing sugar), and briouats (crisp pastry parcels of spiced meat or cheese). The best meals in Marrakesh are often found in unmarked restaurants down small medina alleys, or on the food stalls of Djemaa el-Fna after dark.

Morocco is a Muslim country and alcohol is not widely available in the medina. Wine and beer are served in most hotels and a number of restaurants in Guéliz. Mint tea — sweet, fragrant, poured from a height — is the social lubricant of choice and is offered freely in any shop you enter.
