Scenic

Norway's fjords in
the midnight sun

7 min read  ·  Summer itinerary

In the long Norwegian summer, the sun barely touches the horizon before rising again, bathing mirror-still fjords and cascading waterfalls in a golden light that lasts all night. It is one of the most quietly astonishing experiences in all of European travel.

Why Norway's fjords?

Norway's fjords are the product of glaciers that carved impossibly deep channels into the western coastline over millions of years, leaving behind walls of rock that plunge hundreds of metres straight into the sea. Today UNESCO protects the two finest — Geirangerfjord and Nærøyfjord — as World Heritage landscapes, though the entire region offers a density of natural spectacle that rivals anywhere on Earth.

What sets a summer visit apart is the phenomenon of the midnight sun. Above the Arctic Circle (and in June, even further south), the sun circles the sky without setting, turning ordinary evenings into surreal watercolour panoramas of pink, amber, and pale gold. Hiking a ridge at 11 pm in full daylight, with a fjord glittering 600 metres below, is the kind of experience that defies description and demands to be lived.

Practical tip

Norway's fjord region is best explored by a combination of train, ferry, and bus — collectively marketed as the Norway in a Nutshell route. Book the Flåm Railway and Bergen–Oslo train segments in advance through Vy (the national rail operator). Ferry crossings between fjord villages run frequently in summer and need no advance reservation.

The classic fjord circuit

The Norway in a Nutshell route is the most celebrated self-guided circuit in Scandinavia. From Oslo or Bergen, travellers take the mountain railway across the Hardangervidda plateau, descend the legendary Flåm Railway through twenty tunnels and past towering waterfalls to sea level, cross Aurlandsfjord and Nærøyfjord by passenger ferry, then ride a bus over the mountains to Voss and a final train back to Bergen. The whole loop takes one long day or, far better, two to three days with overnight stops.

Independent travellers willing to stray from the circuit will find quieter arms of the same fjords — kayaking on Sognefjord, cycling the Rallarvegen plateau path, or hiking to the Trolltunga rock formation for a bird's-eye view over Hardangerfjord.

Flåm

A tiny village at the end of the Flåm Railway, where Aurlandsfjord narrows to a thin sliver of steel-blue water. The starting point for most fjord ferry crossings and one of Norway's most visited destinations.

1–2 nights recommended

Bergen

The gateway city of western Norway. Its Bryggen Wharf — a row of coloured wooden warehouses dating to the Hanseatic League — is a UNESCO landmark. Ride the Fløibanen funicular for panoramic views over the seven surrounding mountains.

2 nights recommended

Ålesund

An art nouveau town built on islands in the Sunnmøre archipelago, rebuilt in 1904 after a great fire. The viewpoint at Aksla hill gives one of Norway's finest town panoramas, surrounded by fjord and ocean.

1–2 nights recommended

Geiranger

A tiny village at the head of Geirangerfjord — arguably the world's most beautiful fjord. The Seven Sisters and Suitor waterfalls spill directly into the water. The Eagle Road viewpoint above town is unmissable.

1–2 nights recommended

Best fjords

Geirangerfjord is the most photographed, its sheer 1,400-metre walls draped with waterfalls and dotted with abandoned hillside farms that cling to the cliff-face like afterthoughts. The fjord arm is narrow enough that cruise ships seem impossibly large as they nose between the walls. A RIB speedboat tour in the early morning — before the day-trippers arrive — puts you level with the waterfalls and close enough to feel the spray.

Nærøyfjord, a branch of Sognefjord and the narrowest navigable fjord in Europe, is quieter and arguably more dramatic. At its tightest points, the walls close to barely 250 metres apart, the water turns black with depth, and the scale of the cliffs above induces a kind of joyful vertigo. The two-hour ferry from Gudvangen to Flåm is one of the great short journeys on the continent.

When to go

May to August is the summer window, with June and July offering the most reliable midnight sun experiences. June is particularly magical — the days are impossibly long, the waterfalls are at full roar from snowmelt, and the tourist crowds have not yet peaked. August brings warmer water temperatures (useful if you plan to kayak or swim) and softer evening light as the days begin to shorten.

September sees the first autumn colours transform the valley sides into tapestries of copper and gold — a beautiful season for those who don't need the midnight sun. Winter (December–February) offers the northern lights and a frozen, monochrome version of the fjords that is stark and unforgettable, though most ferry services run reduced schedules.

Where to stay

Bergen offers the widest range of accommodation and makes a natural base for the first and last nights of any fjord trip. In Flåm, the Flåmsbrygga Hotel sits directly on the waterfront and books up months ahead for July. Geiranger's Union Hotel has anchored the village since 1891 and has a heated outdoor pool with fjord views.

For something more unusual, several fjord-side farms rent out traditional Norwegian cabins (hytter) through platforms like Finn.no. Waking to a private fjord view with no other guests in sight is well worth the effort of booking outside the main tourist infrastructure.

Getting around

Norway's Interrail pass covers all main rail routes including the Bergen and Flåm railways. The Ruter and Skyss apps handle regional buses and ferries. Car hire unlocks the most scenic mountain roads — the Trollstigen serpentine and the Sognefjellet mountain road are both best done at your own pace. Petrol stations are frequent, and Norway's charging infrastructure makes electric car hire a genuinely viable option throughout the fjord region.
