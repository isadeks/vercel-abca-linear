Norway's fjords in
the midnight sun

7 min read  ·  Ten-day itinerary

Long, golden evenings on still water, surrounded by walls of granite that plunge straight into the sea. Norway's fjords in summer are among Europe's great natural spectacles — and with the midnight sun stretching each day past midnight, there is almost too much light to take in.

Why Norway's fjords?

The fjords of western Norway are a UNESCO World Heritage landscape — ancient valleys carved by glaciers and then flooded by the sea after the last ice age. The result is a geography of improbable beauty: water so calm it mirrors the mountains above, waterfalls that free-fall hundreds of metres directly into the fjord, and villages clinging to the few flat ledges the terrain allows.

In summer, the midnight sun transforms the light into something continuous and golden. There is no darkness — just a slow, imperceptible dimming around midnight before the sun climbs again. For photographers and those who struggle with sleep, it is simultaneously wonderful and disorienting.

Practical tip

The Norway in a Nutshell route (Bergen → Flåm by Flåmsbana railway → Gudvangen by Nærøyfjord ferry → Voss by bus → Bergen by train) can be done in a single day but deserves at least two. Book ferry and train tickets through NSB or Fjord Tours in advance during peak summer season. Bergen makes an excellent base for exploring the wider region.

The route

A ten-day circuit from Bergen allows you to explore the Nærøyfjord and Sognefjord in depth, drive the Aurlandsvegen (Snow Road) to the Stegastein viewpoint, and still leave time for Ålesund — Norway's most beautiful town, rebuilt in Art Nouveau style after an 1904 fire — and the Geirangerfjord to the north.

Bergen

Norway's second city and the gateway to the fjords. The Bryggen Wharf, Funicular up Mount Fløyen, and the excellent fish market set the tone for what follows. Expect rain — Bergen is one of Europe's wettest cities.

2 nights recommended

Flåm

A tiny village at the inner end of Aurlandsfjord, best reached by the famous Flåmsbana — one of the world's steepest railway lines. A superb base for kayaking, hiking, and fjord cruises.

2 nights recommended

Nærøyfjord

The narrowest fjord in Europe, squeezed between near-vertical walls of rock. A ferry journey through Nærøyfjord is one of the finest boat trips in the world — waterfalls erupt from every crevice overhead.

Day trip from Flåm

Geirangerfjord

The most celebrated fjord of all, with the Seven Sisters and Suitor waterfalls cascading down on either side. The viewpoint at Dalsnibba (1,500 m) provides a perspective that stops conversation entirely.

2 nights recommended

When to go

June to August offers the midnight sun, the warmest temperatures (15–22 °C), and all ferry and mountain road services running. July is peak season — fully booked accommodation and busier viewpoints. June and August are excellent alternatives with noticeably smaller crowds. September brings the first autumn colours to the mountainsides and is one of the most beautiful months to visit, though some routes start closing.

For the Northern Lights, visit between October and March. The fjord experience changes entirely — cold, dark, and sparsely visited, but extraordinarily atmospheric when a clear night brings the aurora dancing over the black water.

Where to stay

Accommodation in the fjord villages ranges from simple guesthouses and historic wooden hotels to spectacular designer lodges cantilevered over the water. In Flåm, the Fretheim Hotel is a charming historic property with fjord views from many rooms. In Geiranger, the Union Hotel has served guests since 1891 and offers one of the most dramatic outlooks in Europe. For something contemporary, the Juvet Landscape Hotel near Ålesund is architecturally extraordinary — glass-fronted rooms positioned to look directly into the forest.

Budget guide

Norway is one of Europe's most expensive countries. Budget travellers staying in hostels and self-catering can manage on NOK 700–1,000 (€60–85) per day. Mid-range visitors should budget NOK 2,000–3,500 (€170–300) per day including accommodation, meals, and ferry tickets. The Norway in a Nutshell full ticket costs approximately NOK 1,500–2,000 (€130–170) per person.

Food & drink

Norwegian food has quietly become one of Scandinavia's most interesting cuisines. Bergen's fish market is the place to try fresh prawns, smoked salmon, and brown crab straight from the boat. In the fjord villages, look for rakfisk (fermented trout) if you're feeling adventurous, or simply enjoy the exceptional quality of local lamb, reindeer, and freshwater fish prepared with minimal fuss.

Coffee culture is serious in Norway — the country consistently ranks among the world's highest per-capita coffee consumers. Expect well-sourced specialty coffee even in small towns. Beer and aquavit (caraway-spiced spirit) are the traditional drinks; the craft beer scene in Bergen is worth exploring on your first evening.
