Adventure

A week at the end
of the world: Patagonia

12 min read  ·  One-week itinerary

Where the Andes dissolve into wind-scoured steppe and shards of glacial ice float in turquoise lakes, Patagonia delivers wilderness on a scale that resets your sense of what the world can be. A week here is barely enough — and utterly unforgettable.

Why Patagonia?

Shared between Argentina and Chile at the southern tip of South America, Patagonia is one of the last truly wild corners of the planet. Its landscapes read like a greatest-hits reel of the natural world: granite spires stabbing through clouds, glaciers calving into fjords, puma-stalked grasslands, and the constant howl of a wind that seems to blow in from the edge of the Earth.

For hikers, this is a pilgrimage destination. For photographers, it is a study in light and scale. For anyone who has felt the itch of the horizon, Patagonia is the answer. A week gives you time to complete the iconic W Trek in Torres del Paine and still savour the slower rhythms of a base town on either side.

Practical tip

Book campsites and refugios on the W Trek months in advance — spots fill up by October for the November–March high season. CONAF (the Chilean national parks authority) requires all trekkers to register online before entering Torres del Paine. Download offline maps on AllTrails or Maps.me before you lose cell signal.

The W Trek route

The W Trek is named for the zigzag shape it traces across the Torres del Paine massif. Most trekkers complete it in four to five days, moving west to east or east to west between three primary valleys: Valle del Francés, Valle del Ascencio (home to the iconic towers), and Glacier Grey. Each arm of the W rewards with a radically different landscape — from hanging glaciers and condor-filled thermals to the rust-red ramparts above Campamento Torres.

The full circuit adds several more days and crosses the remote backside of the massif, but the W alone covers the most celebrated terrain and suits a one-week trip perfectly when combined with travel days on either end.

Torres del Paine

The crown jewel of Chilean Patagonia. The W Trek, Grey Glacier, and the three granite towers at sunrise are the defining experiences of any Patagonia trip.

4–5 days trekking

Los Glaciares

Argentina's answer to Torres del Paine. The Perito Moreno Glacier and Mount Fitz Roy near El Chaltén make this national park an essential companion destination.

2–3 days recommended

Chiloé Island

A rain-soaked archipelago of palafito stilt houses, wooden churches, and mythic folk tradition. A slower, stranger side of Patagonia entirely unlike the mainland parks.

2 days recommended

Tierra del Fuego

The island at the bottom of the world. Ushuaia is the southernmost city on Earth, gateway to Antarctica, and the terminus of the legendary Ruta Nacional 3.

1–2 days recommended

Best base towns

Puerto Natales is the primary gateway to Torres del Paine, sitting 112 km north of the park entrance on the shore of Última Esperanza Sound. Its single main street is lined with gear shops, hostels, and restaurants catering to every budget. Stock up on supplies, rent or buy any missing kit, and spend a rest day exploring the waterfront before or after the trek.

Puerto Montt, further north in Chilean Lake District, serves as the northern gateway for those travelling overland or connecting to Chiloé. It lacks Puerto Natales' charm but has better flight connections and is the departure point for the spectacular three-day Navimag ferry south through the fjords — one of the great slow-travel journeys in South America.

When to go

November to March is the Patagonian summer and the only realistic window for the W Trek. January and February are peak season — busiest on trail, warmest temperatures (10–18 °C), but most reliable weather. November and March offer thinner crowds and lower prices at the cost of more unpredictable conditions.

Patagonian weather is famously volatile at any time of year. Sun, rain, hail, and gale-force wind can arrive within the same hour. Layer aggressively, carry waterproofs at all times, and treat any calm blue morning as a gift rather than a given.

Where to stay

Inside Torres del Paine, the W Trek is serviced by a chain of refugios (staffed mountain huts offering dormitory beds and hot meals) and designated campsites. Reservations through Vertice Patagonia or Las Torres Patagonia are essential and open up to a year in advance. Free camping is not permitted in the park.

In Puerto Natales, options range from budget dorms at sociable hiker hostels to smart boutique hotels with lake views. For a splurge, the Singular Patagonia — a converted cold-storage plant on the Última Esperanza — is one of the finest hotels in South America.

Gear essentials

A waterproof shell jacket rated for sustained wind is non-negotiable. Trekking poles reduce knee strain on the steep descents into each valley. Gaiters keep feet dry on muddy trail sections. Pack a down or synthetic insulated layer even in summer — temperatures drop sharply after dark in camp. Bring trekking poles, a headlamp, and a two-litre water bottle; water is safe to drink straight from the rivers and streams throughout the park.
